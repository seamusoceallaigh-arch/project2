1)  Meeting the brief

The artefact I developed is a wildfire risk assessment system using a BBC micro:bit. The purpose of the system is to monitor environmental conditions and estimate the potential risk of a wildfire. It collects environmental data using sensors and processes the information to display a warning when wildfire risk increases.

The system uses several input devices. The built-in temperature sensor measures surrounding heat levels, while the light sensor detects the amount of light in the environment, which can indicate hot and dry conditions that increase wildfire risk. In addition, a soil moisture sensor connected to the micro:bit measures how wet or dry the soil is, which is an important indicator of fire risk in forest environments.

The micro:bit processes this data using a programmed algorithm. The system continuously reads the sensor values and compares them with predefined threshold levels. These thresholds represent environmental conditions that may increase the likelihood of a wildfire, such as high temperatures, strong light, and low soil moisture. Based on these readings, the program calculates a risk level and determines whether conditions indicate low, medium, or high wildfire risk.

The output of the system is displayed on the micro:bit LED display and through sound alerts. When environmental readings remain within safe limits, the display indicates that wildfire risk is low. If the sensor readings exceed the thresholds, the system provides a warning signal to indicate increased wildfire risk. The system also includes user interaction through the micro:bit buttons, allowing users to start or stop data logging or reset the stored data.

To meet the advanced requirements, I developed a Python disaster risk model using environmental data collected from the micro:bit. The recorded data was exported as CSV files and analysed using Python with the pandas and matplotlib libraries. The model normalised temperature, moisture, and light values and calculated a weighted wildfire risk score.

I then tested two “what-if” scenarios to evaluate how changing environmental conditions affect wildfire risk. The first scenario simulated a heatwave, where higher temperatures increased the predicted wildfire risk. The second scenario simulated heavy rainfall, where increased moisture levels reduced the predicted wildfire risk.

Overall, the artefact demonstrates environmental data collection, automated risk assessment, and predictive modelling for wildfire risk.

2)  Investigation

Forests are vital ecosystems that provide clean air, store carbon, protect biodiversity, and help regulate water and temperature. However, wildfires are a significant threat, destroying large areas of forest each year and affecting the environment, economy, and human health. While natural factors such as drought, wind, and lightning can influence wildfire occurrence, human activity is also a major cause. Because of this, understanding and predicting wildfire risk is important in order to reduce environmental damage and respond quickly when fires occur.

Several real-world systems have been developed to detect and monitor forest fires. One example is ARNOWA’s forest management solution, which uses a network of smart sensors combined with AI-based image and video analysis to monitor forests in real time. This system can detect forest fires and illegal logging and generate alerts so that authorities can respond quickly. However, although ARNOWA provides advanced monitoring, it requires large infrastructure, specialised technology, and significant financial investment. This makes it expensive and difficult to deploy on a small scale, and it is not suitable for educational or small prototype projects. (Reference3)  

Because of these limitations, a micro:bit-based wildfire early warning and prediction system provides a more accessible alternative. The system measures environmental variables such as temperature, soil moisture, and light intensity to estimate wildfire risk in a local area. Temperature readings can indicate heat conditions that increase fire risk, soil moisture helps determine how dry the ground is, and light intensity can influence both soil dryness and temperature. By combining these variables, the system can estimate wildfire risk more effectively than relying on only one measurement.

Another advantage of using a micro:bit is that it allows practical experimentation and learning. It helps develop skills in embedded systems, sensor data collection, and risk modelling. Compared to large commercial monitoring systems, the micro:bit solution is low-cost, portable, and suitable for classroom use while still demonstrating how wildfire detection systems work.

In conclusion, investigating existing solutions such as ARNOWA helped me inform the design choices for this project. While large monitoring systems are effective, they are complex and expensive. The micro:bit-based system was chosen because it is affordable, simple to implement, and suitable for demonstrating the core principles of wildfire monitoring and prediction in a classroom environment.

3. Plan and design

Design Objectives

The main aim of this project was to design a wildfire risk monitoring system that could collect real environmental data and use it to predict possible wildfire conditions. I wanted to build something that combined a physical embedded system with a computer-based model.

The main objectives were:

  • To collect temperature, light and soil moisture data using sensors.
  • To simulate wildfire risk based on these environmental factors.
  • To create a Python model that uses the collected data.
  • To test two “what-if” scenarios.
  • To include an automatic alert system when risk becomes high.
  • To make sure the system runs automatically once started.

Considering Different Options

At the planning stage, I considered four different project ideas.

Option1: smart forest irrigation system

This project involved using a soil moisture sensor to automatically activate watering when dryness levels increased. While this option met the embedded system requirement, It was not selected because it focused mainly on plant maintenance

I chose to develop a wildfire risk assessment system (option 4 ) because wildfire risk is strongly affected by temperature and dryness, which can be measured using micro:bit sensors. This makes it suitable for an embedded system that collects real environmental data and uses it to model disaster risk. The system can analyse the data to predict different risk levels and trigger alerts visually and audible when conditions become dangerous. It also matches the project brief’s focus on forest disasters and is safe to build and test in a supervised classroom environment.

Justification of Design Choices

I chose the BBC micro:bit as my embedded hardware because it has built-in sensors like a temperature sensor and light sensor, which makes data collection simple and reliable. It also has programmable buttons for digital input and supports outputs like the LED display and GPIO pins. I used the micro:bit buttons to start or calibrate the system, and the built-in temperature sensor to measure environmental conditions linked to wildfire risk. For outputs, I used the LED matrix to show different risk levels with symbols, and an external buzzer to give an audible warning during high-risk conditions.I programmed the embedded system using Microsoft MakeCode because it makes it easy to read sensors and create automatic loops. I also used Python on a computer to create the disaster risk model, as it allows data analysis and testing different scenarios. A simple threshold-based risk scoring system was chosen so the results are clear and easy to understand.

Stakeholders and End Users

This system could be useful for:

  • Forestry services
  • Environmental protection agencies
  • Farmers and landowners
  • Communities living near forests

These groups would benefit from early warnings and predictions of wildfire risk. Although my system is a classroom prototype, it represents how real monitoring systems could help reduce environmental damage.

Technologies Used

I used the BBC micro:bit as the embedded platform because it already includes built-in temperature and light sensors. It also supports Microsoft Makecode and allows external components like a buzzer to be connected.

4. Create

The development of my wildfire risk detection system progressed in several key stages. The first milestone was designing and programming the embedded system logic using the BBC micro:bit in MakeCode. I configured the system to collect  environmental data using both analogue and digital inputs. The soil moisture sensor was connected to analogue pin P0, while temperature and light intensity were collected using the built-in micro:bit sensors. I programmed the system to continuously read these values once started. Using conditional (if–else) statements, I created a variable called fireRisk, which calculated a risk level between 1 and 3. The thresholds were designed so that:

  • High temperature + low soil moisture + high light intensity = Risk Level 3
  • Moderate conditions = Risk Level 2
  • Cooler temperature + higher moisture = Risk Level 1

This allowed the system to simulate wildfire risk based on real environmental conditions, fulfilling the process simulation requirement of the brief.

The second milestone was integrating outputs so the system could respond automatically. When fireRisk was equal to 3, the micro:bit displayed a warning icon and played an alarm sound. When fireRisk was 2, it displayed a different icon and tone, and when fireRisk was 1, the system remained in a safe state. This fulfilled the adaptive requirement, as the system responded automatically to environmental changes.

The third milestone involved data logging. I enabled logging so that temperature, moisture, light, and fireRisk values were stored over time. This dataset was later exported as a CSV file and used in the Python modelling stage.

The fourth milestone was developing the disaster risk model in Python using Thonny. I imported the CSV dataset and built a weighted wildfire risk score system. First, I normalised the variables so that temperature, soil moisture, and light intensity were scaled consistently. I then applied weighted values:

  • Temperature weight =  0.4
  • Soil moisture weight = 0.4
  • Light intensity weight = 0.2

The weighted risk score was calculated using:

Risk Score = (0.4 × Temperature) + (0.4 × Dryness) + (0.2 × Light)

This allowed the model to produce a continuous risk score rather than just fixed thresholds. The risk score was then classified into levels 1, 2, or 3.

During testing I carried out a combination of functional, simulation, scenario-based, and system testing throughout my project. I used functional testing to check that the sensors, risk calculation, and outputs were working correctly. I used simulation testing by recreating real-world conditions, such as dry soil, high temperature, and strong light using a phone torch.

I also carried out scenario-based testing in my Python model by creating heatwave and rainfall ‘what-if’ simulations. Finally, I used system testing to make sure that the micro:bit, data logging, and Python model all worked together correctly.

One significant problem encountered was that the what-if scenario simulations initially did not run. After debugging, I discovered that the plt.show() function in Matplotlib was blocking execution, preventing the scenario code from running. I solved this by moving the scenario simulations above the graph plotting section. This allowed the model to calculate and display the scenario risk distributions correctly.

Another issue I encountered was with the light intensity data. Although the light sensor showed correct readings during live testing, the values appeared as zero in the exported CSV file. I realised this happened because the phone torch I used to simulate light was too far away during logging, so the sensor did not record the light properly. To overcome this, I simulated and recalculated the light values directly in the Python model for the what-if scenarios. This allowed the disaster risk model to still analyse the impact of increased or decreased light intensity, even though some of the original logged values were inaccurate.

In the final milestone I created two what-if simulations to meet the advanced requirements. The first simulation was a heatwave scenario where I increased temperature and light intensity. The model classified all data points as high wildfire risk. This demonstrated predictive analysis under extreme climate conditions. The second simulation was heavy rainfall where I reduced soil dryness and sunlight, which resulted in mostly low and medium risk classifications. This showed how increased moisture significantly reduces wildfire probability. These simulations demonstrated how the model could predict outcomes under different environmental scenarios.

In conclusion, my development process involved planning, implementation, testing, debugging, and refinement to ensure the system functioned correctly and met both the basic and advanced requirements of the brief.

5. Evaluation

My final artefact successfully meets both the basic and advanced requirements of the project brief. The embedded system using the BBC micro:bit collected environmental data including temperature, soil moisture, and light intensity, and simulated a wildfire risk process. The system operated automatically once started and produced adaptive outputs such as visual indicators and sound alerts when high fire risk was detected. Testing showed that the system responded correctly to dry soil and high temperature conditions by increasing the fire risk level.

The Python disaster model extended the embedded system by analysing the collected dataset and calculating a weighted wildfire risk score. The model allowed predictive analysis through two what-if simulations. In the heatwave scenario, increasing temperature and sunlight resulted in all data points being classified as high risk. In the heavy rainfall scenario, lowering soil dryness and light intensity reduced the overall wildfire risk. These results demonstrate that the model behaves logically and adapts to changing environmental conditions.

One limitation of the project was that the data was collected indoors, meaning light values did not vary significantly. Additionally, the soil moisture sensor provides relative readings rather than precise percentage values. Despite this, the system still demonstrated realistic wildfire behaviour patterns.

If I were to improve this project, I would leave the system outdoors for longer-term data collection. I would also add sensors such as humidity or wind speed which can affect wildfire risk. Overall, my project effectively demonstrates environmental monitoring, modelling, and adaptive response.
